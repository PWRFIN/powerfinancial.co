Section 01
The IUL Sales Process
A full IUL sale is typically 2–3 hours across 2–3 touchpoints. It is NOT a one-call close — though it can be. Know the flow before you get on the phone.
⚡ Golden Rule Before We Start

Discovery = qualification, not persuasion. Strategy comes before illustrations. Numbers come after strategy. If you reverse this order, you will lose the sale — and you may end up with a policy that's wrong for the client.

💰 How Commission Works on IUL Policies

Understanding how you get paid helps you design better policies and manage client conversations around premium.

The Two Numbers: Target vs. AP

Commission is based on the lower of two amounts: the Target Premium or the Annualized Premium (AP).

Target Premium — a carrier-set amount based on the client's age and the death benefit amount. It's the "sweet spot" premium the carrier uses as their commission baseline.

AP (Annualized Premium) — what the client actually pays annually (monthly × 12).

Commission is calculated on whichever is lower. In most cases, commission is paid on the Target.

How Target Is Calculated

Target premium is set by the carrier based on: (1) Client's age + (2) Death benefit amount. Older clients = higher target. Higher death benefit = higher target.

You can see the Target column in the illustration spreadsheet. Never show this column to a client. It is an internal reference only.

📌If a client is underfunding (paying less than Target), you earn less commission than the max. Max commission = AP ≥ Target. This is why funding level matters — for the client AND for you.

Section 03
Strategy Decision Logic
Two options. One question decides it: Does the client primarily want death benefit coverage, or do they want to grow / access / leverage money?
The Single Question That Decides Strategy

"What matters more to you right now: protecting your family if you die, or building a living asset you can use while you're alive?"

If they say protection → Option A. If they say living asset / growth / retirement / banking → Option B. If both → Hybrid, lean B with A-style face amount discussion.

Option A
Death Benefit Focus
Designed to maximize the death benefit per dollar of premium. Protection-first design. Lower cash value growth. Good for income replacement, mortgage protection, estate planning, or FEX-style budget-conscious coverage.
  • Client wants maximum DB per dollar
  • Legacy / wealth transfer is #1 goal
  • Mortgage protection
  • Budget-conscious — lower premium
Option B — Cash Value / Retirement Income
Living Benefits Focus
Designed to maximize cash value accumulation and tax-free access. This is the IUL as a financial vehicle — "be your own banker," supplement retirement income, "million dollar baby" child policies, borrowing power, college funding.
  • Build wealth / grow money tax-deferred
  • Tax-free retirement income (age 65–90)
  • "Be your own banker" / borrowing power
  • Million Dollar Baby (child policy)

Trigger: Client has a Traditional IRA or SEP IRA (self-employed)
"A traditional IRA gives you a deduction now but you pay taxes later — on everything, including all the growth. If your tax rate is higher when you retire (which it often is), you end up paying more. A SEP is powerful for self-employed people — up to $69,000 a year — but it follows the same rule: every dollar coming out is taxed."
"Traditional IRAs and many retirement plans require minimum distributions beginning at age 73 or 75, depending on your birth year, regardless of whether you need the income. These withdrawals are generally taxable. A properly structured Indexed Universal Life (IUL) policy offers an alternative approach to supplemental retirement income. Unlike traditional tax-deferred retirement accounts, IUL policies have no required minimum distributions, allowing policyholders greater flexibility over when and how they access available cash value through withdrawals or policy loans, subject to policy terms and tax rules."
Trigger: "I just keep money in savings" or "I have CDs"
"Savings accounts and CDs are safe — FDIC insured. But the interest is taxable every year, rates are low relative to inflation, and money sitting in a savings account is actually losing purchasing power over time. There's no growth strategy there."
~4–5% current HYSA/CD rate — but fully taxable 3.4% average inflation — erodes real return 0% floor IUL gives you growth participation with no loss from index decline
"The IUL is not a replacement for your emergency fund — keep 3–6 months liquid in savings. But any dollar beyond that? It could be working harder, growing tax-deferred, and eventually producing tax-free income instead of sitting flat."
Trigger: "I have life insurance through work"
"Employer life insurance is a great benefit — but it's not really yours. It follows your job. The minute you change employers, get laid off, or retire, that coverage can disappear. And it's usually only 1–2 times your salary — which sounds like a lot until you do the math."
"If you make $60,000 a year and have 1x coverage, your family gets $60,000. That's one year of income. If you have dependents, a mortgage, kids in college — one year doesn't cut it. And it builds zero cash value."
Trigger: Client doesn't have a retirement plan, or is worried about running out of money
"The biggest financial risk most Americans face today isn't dying too soon — it's living too long. The average retirement now lasts 20–30 years. Social Security was never designed to be a primary income source, and most people's 401k balance will run out in years, not decades."
"A 40% market loss doesn't take 40% to recover — it takes about 67%. If your portfolio drops 40% the year before you retire, you need a 67% gain just to break even. With an IUL, when markets go down, you don't. Your floor is 0%. Your upside is capped — but your downside is eliminated. Someone who retired during the 2008 financial crisis could have needed approximately 4–6 years for their stock portfolio to recover, assuming they remained invested and didn't withdraw money. But there's a major distinction between the stock market recovering and a retiree's portfolio recovering. For someone who retired in 2008 and immediately began withdrawing income, recovery could have taken considerably longer—or might never have happened. This is one of the most important concepts in retirement planning: sequence-of-returns risk."
Trigger: Client carries significant debt or is interested in the "banking" concept
"Think about a traditional bank: if you have $100,000 and withdraw $40,000, only the remaining $60,000 continues earning interest. With an IUL using participating loans, you can borrow against your cash value while the full $100,000 may remain eligible to earn indexed interest. However, policy loans are not free withdrawals. The insurance company charges interest on the borrowed amount, and the outstanding loan reduces your available net cash value and death benefit. If not properly managed, loans can also jeopardize the policy. The advantage is that you may access funds while your underlying cash value continues working for you, subject to policy terms and charges."
Trigger: Client has a mortgage, new homeowner, or mentions wanting to protect their home
"Most people think about mortgage protection as a simple term policy — it pays off the house if you die. That's it. An IUL does all of that AND builds an asset you can access while you're alive. So instead of paying for protection that expires in 20 years with zero residual value, you're paying for protection that grows into a financial resource. Furthermore, you can leverage the cash value in an IUL to help pay for the mortgage when you can't, or you can use it to pay the house off sooner, saving thousands in interest."
Trigger: Client has children and wants to plan for college costs
"529 plans are the most common college savings tool, and they're decent — but they have a major limitation: if your kid doesn't go to college, or gets a scholarship, that money is stuck. Pull it out for non-education use and you pay taxes AND a 10% penalty. Plus, 529 assets count against financial aid calculations."
"An IUL doesn't count as an asset in most federal financial aid formulas. The cash value is accessible for college — or for a wedding, a business, a car, anything. No penalties, no restrictions. And if your kid doesn't use it for college, it just keeps growing for retirement."
$200k+ average 4-year private college cost (2025+) 0% penalty IUL — access cash for any reason Not counted as asset in FAFSA for most IUL structures
Trigger: Client has a growing family, dependents, or is actively planning their family's financial security
Problem: Protecting Your Family's Financial Future

Raising a family comes with growing financial responsibilities—mortgages, childcare, education, and everyday expenses. If a parent passes away unexpectedly, their family could lose essential income, leaving loved ones struggling to maintain their lifestyle and meet long-term financial goals.

IUL Solution: Protection Today, Flexibility for Tomorrow

An Indexed Universal Life (IUL) policy provides a death benefit to help protect your family's financial security while offering the potential to accumulate cash value over time. That cash value may be accessed through withdrawals or policy loans to help fund future needs, such as education, emergencies, or major family expenses. It's life insurance designed to provide protection today with potential financial flexibility for tomorrow, subject to policy terms, costs, and loan risks.

Trigger: Client has a child under 18 / wants to set their child up for financial success
"The most powerful financial gift a parent can give a child isn't a savings account or a 529 — it's locking in permanent life insurance while they're young, healthy, and before any medical history develops. This is called a juvenile policy, and it does something no other tool can: it guarantees insurability for life."
9 days old earliest most carriers will insure $25–$100/mo typical starting premium range Usually a higher DB than adults start with Locked in forever — no new underwriting ever needed
Trigger: Client actively uses Robinhood, Acorns, Webull, or similar retail investing apps
Problem: Investing Without Financial Protection

Many people use apps like Robinhood to invest in stocks, ETFs, and other securities, hoping to build wealth over time. While these platforms offer opportunities for growth, investments are exposed to market volatility and potential losses. A significant market downturn could reduce their savings just when they need them most. Additionally, a brokerage account generally doesn't provide life insurance protection.

IUL Solution: Growth Potential With Downside Protection

An Indexed Universal Life (IUL) policy can complement traditional investments by providing life insurance protection while offering the potential for cash value accumulation linked to market index performance. Unlike directly investing in stocks, IUL indexed accounts generally offer a 0% floor against negative index returns, although policy charges can still reduce cash value. This allows clients to diversify their financial strategies, protect their loved ones, and build a potential source of supplemental income without directly exposing their indexed cash value to stock market losses.

Section 07
Objection Responses
Mirror the objection — never argue. Validate the concern, then redirect with facts. Numbers over emotion. Stay calm. Silence is fine.
I already have life insurance through work
▶
Your Response
That's a great benefit — I'd never take that away from you. But here's what employer coverage can't do: it's not yours. It follows your job. If you switch employers, get laid off, or retire, it can disappear overnight. It also typically only covers 1–2x salary — which is often less than one year of your family's income. And it builds zero cash value you can access while you're alive. This is about complementing what you have, not replacing it.
I can't afford it right now
▶
Your Response
I understand — that's the same thing I thought before I understood it. But let me show you the cost of waiting. Every year you delay is a year of compounding you can never recover. At 30, $200/month builds dramatically more than the same $200 at 40 — not a little more, dramatically more. There are options starting at $25–50/month. What's a number that does feel possible right now? Let me show you what even a small amount looks like over time.
I'll just invest in the stock market / index funds
▶
Your Response
Index funds are a great tool — I won't argue with that. But here's the difference: (1) Your index fund is fully exposed to losses — when the market drops 40%, your account drops 40%. A 40% loss requires a 67% gain just to break even. The IUL has a 0% floor — you don't participate in losses. (2) Your gains in a brokerage account are taxable — capital gains, dividends, all of it. IUL growth is tax-deferred, and distributions through policy loans are tax-free. (3) Your investment has no death benefit. It builds no protection. This isn't either/or — it's both. But the IUL covers risks your index fund doesn't.
What if I lose money in it?
▶
Your Response
That's actually one of the biggest misunderstandings about IUL — and it's a fair concern. Your cash value cannot decrease because of index performance. The floor is 0%. In a year when the market drops 30%, your policy credits 0% — not -30%. You don't participate in the loss. Monthly charges are still deducted — so the cash value can dip slightly from fees in very early years — but you are never exposed to market downturns. What you lose, you lose only to the ceiling (cap) on the upside. What you don't lose is the downside.
I heard IULs are complicated and have hidden fees
▶
Your Response
That's fair — and there are agents who overcomplicate this. My job is to make it simple. At its core: you pay a premium, a portion goes to insurance cost, the rest builds cash value that grows tied to a market index with a floor and a cap. Yes, there are internal charges — cost of insurance, admin fees. These are disclosed in the illustration. The question isn't "are there fees" — every financial product has fees. The question is: net of fees, what does this produce compared to your alternatives? Let's look at that comparison directly.
I need to think about it / I need to talk to my spouse
▶
Your Response
Of course — I'd never push a decision like this. What specific part do you want to think through? Sometimes there's one piece that isn't clear yet, and I can address it right now so your conversation with your spouse is easier. [Listen for the real objection.] If they genuinely need a spouse conversation: "Would it help to schedule a call with both of you together? I can walk through everything in 30 minutes and you can both ask questions at the same time."
My financial advisor told me IULs are bad
▶
Your Response
I respect your advisor. There's a common bias in the investment world against insurance products — partly because financial advisors often don't earn commissions on insurance, partly because IULs done wrong are actually a bad product. The key is how it's designed and who designs it. What I'd ask you: has your advisor ever specifically analyzed the tax-free income component of a properly structured IUL versus your current tax exposure in retirement? That's usually where the conversation changes. This isn't about replacing your advisor's strategy — it's about tax diversification they may not have on their menu.
What if the insurance company goes out of business?
▶
Your Response
That's a real concern — and there's a real protection for it. Every state has a guaranty association that protects policyholders if an insurer becomes insolvent — typically up to $300,000–$500,000 in cash value and death benefits. Additionally, the carriers I work with are rated A or better by AM Best — companies with decades of claims-paying history and billions in assets. But the safeguard exists for worst-case scenarios. You're more protected here than a bank deposit over $250,000.
I want to wait until my financial situation improves
▶
Your Response
I understand that instinct — I really do. But let me show you something: the cost of a policy goes UP every year you wait, because you're older and insurance pricing is based on age and health. Whatever the premium is today, it will never be lower than today. Also — what if your health changes while you're waiting? The only thing that locks in your insurability is starting the policy. I'm not saying fund it at the maximum. I'm saying start it, even at $50–100 a month, to secure your age and health class now.
I don't believe in life insurance — it's a scam
▶
Your Response
Tell me more about that — because you're right that some types of insurance are a bad deal. Term insurance that expires with nothing to show for it — I understand the frustration. But what if I showed you a policy where the majority of your money comes back to you, with tax-free interest, during your lifetime? This isn't betting on death. This is a living financial asset that happens to also have a death benefit attached. Let me show you a 20-year comparison of what goes in versus what comes out — and you can decide if that fits your definition of a scam.
I heard IULs are a scam designed to pay agents huge commissions
▶
Your Response

That's a fair concern — I'm glad you brought it up so I can be transparent. Yes, agents are compensated on IUL policies. So are financial advisors on mutual funds, Realtors on home sales, and doctors on procedures. Compensation doesn't make something a scam — misrepresentation does. What you should know: commission comes from the carrier, not your premium. Your money goes into the policy regardless. My incentive to do right by you is simple — clients with well-designed policies stay and refer others. Clients with bad ones don't. Would you like me to show you the numbers on paper so you can judge it yourself?

IULs are too complicated — I don't understand how they work
▶
Your Response

You're right that IULs have moving parts — and anyone who says they're simple isn't being honest with you. But you don't need to understand every gear in your car's engine to drive it safely. Here's the three-sentence version: your premium goes into an account that earns interest tied to the market — but you can never lose money when the market drops. You can borrow from it tax-free while you're alive. And your family gets a death benefit when you pass. The complexity exists to protect you legally and tax-wise. I'm here to translate all of it. What specific part feels most confusing right now?

I'd love an IUL but I can't afford the amount I want to put in
▶
Your Response

The most important thing isn't the amount — it's starting. An IUL is flexible, not a fixed payment like a car loan. You can start at $50 or $100 a month, lock in your age and health today, and increase your premium when your income grows. Every year you wait, you're paying for coverage at an older age — and that cost never comes down. Let me show you what even $75 a month looks like over 20 years. Then as things improve, you simply increase it. The right amount today is whatever you can actually sustain. What feels realistic right now?

My spouse/partner needs to be involved and they're skeptical
▶
Your Response

Completely reasonable — this is a household financial decision and both people should understand it. I actually prefer when both partners are in the conversation because it avoids misunderstandings later. Would it help if we scheduled a 30-minute call with both of you together? I'll walk through everything from scratch, answer both of your questions, and there's zero pressure. The goal is that you both feel confident — not that one of you got sold something. What's a time that works for both of you?

What if I miss a payment or need to stop paying?
▶
Your Response

This is one of the advantages of an IUL over whole life — it's flexible. If you go through a financial stretch and need to reduce or pause your premium, the cash value inside the policy can cover the monthly charges for a period of time, keeping the policy active. It's not a fixed commitment. Now — if the cash value runs out and you've stopped paying, the policy could lapse, which is why we design it with adequate funding. But it's built to bend rather than break. I can also show you the no-lapse guarantee rider option that adds a safety net. Does that help?

I already have term insurance — isn't that enough?
▶
Your Response

Term is great for what it does — it's affordable protection for a specific window. The question is: what happens when the term expires? If you're still alive at 65 and your 20-year term ends, you have no coverage and you'd be buying new coverage at an older age and much higher cost. An IUL doesn't expire. It builds value while you're alive and pays your family when you pass — no matter when that is. Term protects against dying too soon. An IUL also protects against living longer than your savings. They serve different purposes. The question is whether you want coverage that pays only if you die young, or coverage that also builds an asset you can use while you're alive.

Section 12
Industry Glossary
Know these terms cold. You should be able to explain every one of them to a client in plain English without hesitating.
0-9
7-Pay Premium / 7-Pay Test
IRS test to determine MEC status. A policy becomes a MEC if total premiums paid exceed the sum of 7 equal annual premiums that would fully pay up the policy. The "Max Allowed" column in your spreadsheet is this number × 7 years ÷ 7 = annual amount. Fund below this to preserve tax-free loan status.
A
Accumulated Value
The total cash accumulation component of the policy after all charges and credits — before surrender charges or loans. This is the "account balance" inside the policy.
Actuarial Guidelines (AG49 / AG49-A)
Regulations issued by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) that govern how IUL policies can be illustrated to consumers. AG49 (2015) capped the maximum illustrated crediting rate based on historical index performance. AG49-A (2020) added further restrictions on multiplier bonuses and illustrated loan arbitrage. The result: illustrations must be conservative enough to reflect realistic outcomes. When clients ask why the illustration 'looks conservative,' AG49-A is the reason.
AML (Anti-Money Laundering)
Federal compliance requirement. Insurance companies must verify that premiums come from legitimate sources. You'll need income, net worth, and employer info from every applicant.
C
Cap Rate
The maximum interest rate that can be credited to your policy in a given segment period. If the index grows 18% but the cap is 10%, you receive 10%. Caps are set by the carrier and can change annually — though changes only take effect at the start of a new segment.
Cash Surrender Value (CSV)
What the policyholder receives if they fully surrender the policy — accumulated value minus surrender charges and any outstanding loans. Different from accumulated value especially in early years.
COI (Cost of Insurance)
The monthly charge for the pure insurance protection component. Based on age, gender, health class, and net amount at risk (NAR). Increases as the insured gets older.
Compound Interest
Interest calculated on the original principal AND all previously accumulated interest. The formula is: A = P(1 + r)^t. This is how IUL cash value grows — each year's interest becomes part of next year's base. At 7% compound over 30 years, $10,000 becomes ~$76,000. At 3% simple interest over the same period, it stays ~$19,000.
Corridor
IRS-required minimum gap between the death benefit and the cash value. If cash value grows large enough to violate the corridor, the carrier automatically increases the death benefit. This keeps the policy classified as life insurance (not an investment).
CVAT (Cash Value Accumulation Test)
One of two IRS tests for qualifying as life insurance. Allows more premium early on. Often used for single-premium or short-pay designs. Usually paired with Option A death benefit.
D
Death Benefit Option A (Level)
Death benefit stays at the stated face amount. Lower cost of insurance since NAR decreases as cash value grows. Used during distribution phase or for protection-focused designs.
Death Benefit Option B (Increasing)
Death benefit = face amount PLUS accumulated cash value. Higher COI (since NAR stays consistent) but allows more premium to flow in under GPT. Used during accumulation phase for cash value designs. Switch to Option A when distributions begin.
DEFRA (Deficit Reduction Act, 1984)
The federal legislation that established the formal definition of 'life insurance' under IRS Section 7702, introducing the GPT (Guideline Premium Test) and CVAT (Cash Value Accumulation Test) as the two qualifying tests.
DIFF Column
In the illustration spreadsheet — the difference between what a client is paying and the maximum allowed premium. Shows how much room they have to increase contributions without becoming a MEC.
E
Equity Index
A market index that tracks the performance of a group of stocks. Examples: S&P 500 (500 largest U.S. companies), Nasdaq-100 (top 100 non-financial), Russell 2000 (small-cap). In an IUL, your cash value doesn't invest in these stocks — it earns credited interest based on how the index performs, subject to a floor (minimum 0%) and a cap (maximum crediting rate).
F
Face Amount / Death Benefit
The base amount of life insurance coverage on the policy — what the carrier pays to beneficiaries upon the insured's death. Also called 'face amount,' 'stated death benefit,' or 'specified amount.'
FEX Policy
Final Expense — a small face amount policy designed for budget-conscious clients. In the IUL context, structuring "like a FEX" means prioritizing protection over cash value, with a lower, sustainable premium.
Fixed Account
An allocation option within the IUL that earns a declared fixed interest rate (typically 2–3%). Not tied to any index. Used as a safe holding account or for stability in the allocation mix.
Flat Extra
An additional charge per $1,000 of death benefit applied when the insured has a health condition or engages in activities that exceed standard risk. Added on top of the standard table rating.
Flexible Premium
A defining feature of Universal Life Insurance (including IUL) that allows the policyholder to vary the amount, timing, and frequency of premium payments — within carrier limits and IRS guidelines (GPT/CVAT).
Floor
The minimum interest credited in any segment. On most IUL products, the floor is 0% — meaning if the index drops 30%, you are credited 0%, not -30%. Your principal is protected from index losses.
G
GLP (Guideline Level Premium)
Under GPT — the maximum annual premium that can be paid in any policy year. This is the "Max Allowed" column in your illustration spreadsheet. Must not be exceeded or policy may become a MEC.
GPT (Guideline Premium Test)
The most commonly used IRS test for life insurance qualification. Sets maximum premium limits based on the death benefit. The Guideline Level Premium (GLP) is the max you can pay annually. Best for regular premium designs aimed at cash value growth with retirement income distributions.
GSP (Guideline Single Premium)
Under GPT — the maximum total (lifetime) premium allowed. Relevant if a client wants to make a large lump-sum contribution. Can never exceed this amount total.
H
Hedge Fund
A pooled private investment vehicle used by high-net-worth individuals and institutions. The IUL connection: the way a carrier funds index crediting is conceptually similar to hedging — the carrier invests premium in safe bonds and uses a small portion to buy index options. This replicates upside participation (capped) without downside exposure.
I
Index (in the context of IUL)
A benchmark that tracks the performance of a group of assets. In IUL, your cash value growth is tied to the performance of an external index. You are NOT invested in the index — the insurance company uses options to replicate the index's performance.
Index Fund
A mutual fund or ETF that passively tracks a market index like the S&P 500. Unlike an IUL, index funds have NO floor: a 30% crash means a 30% loss. No death benefit, no tax-free loan access, and gains are taxable.
Index Loan
A policy loan type where the collateral (loaned amount) remains in the indexed account and continues earning indexed credits. When index returns exceed the loan interest rate, the net cost is positive — you earn more than you're charged.
Insurable Interest
A legal requirement that the policy owner must have a recognized financial or relationship stake in the insured person's life. Required at policy issue. Parent over child = automatic. Spouses = automatic.
IRS Section 7702
The section of the Internal Revenue Code that defines what qualifies as a 'life insurance contract' for tax purposes. A policy must pass either the GPT or CVAT to qualify. Section 7702 is the legal foundation for every tax benefit an IUL offers: tax-deferred growth, tax-free policy loans, and income-tax-free death benefit.
L
Lapse / Policy Lapse
When a policy terminates because the cash value is insufficient to cover monthly charges. A lapsed policy with outstanding loans can trigger a taxable event — the loan balance becomes taxable income.
Loan Credit
The amount of fixed and/or indexed interest credited to the portion of current value that is linked to a loan. In participating loan structures, this credit reduces or eliminates the net borrowing cost.
M
MEC (Modified Endowment Contract)
A policy that has been overfunded beyond IRS limits in the first 7 years. Once a MEC, all distributions (loans and withdrawals) are taxed as ordinary income and subject to 10% penalty before age 59½.
MIB (Medical Information Bureau)
A shared database used by insurance carriers to check an applicant's prior medical and insurance application history. Honesty in applications is essential — discrepancies are flagged.
N
NAR (Net Amount at Risk)
The difference between the current death benefit and the current accumulated value. The insurance company is "at risk" for this amount.
No Lapse Guarantee (NLG)
A rider that guarantees the policy stays in force — even if the cash value is zero — as long as minimum required premiums are paid.
P
Participating Interest
An interest crediting method where the policy participates in the gains of an indexed strategy even on funds that are currently out on loan.
Participating Loan / Wash Loan
A loan where the policy credits interest on the collateral at the same rate (or near the same rate) charged on the loan — resulting in a near-zero net cost of borrowing.
Participation Rate
The percentage of index growth that is credited to your policy. A 100% participation rate means you receive 100% of the (capped) index growth.
Point-to-Point Crediting
The most common crediting method. Interest is calculated by comparing the index value at the START of the segment to the value at the END (one year later). Only the change over the full year is used — intra-year volatility is ignored.
Policy Maturity
The date when the insurance contract ends and the full accumulated value is paid to the policyholder. For modern IULs, this is age 120 or 121.
Premium Call
A notice from the carrier that additional premium is required to keep the policy in force. Occurs when cash value can no longer cover monthly charges.
Q
Qualified vs. Non-Qualified Accounts
Qualified accounts (401k, IRA, 403b, SEP): contributions are pre-tax; all withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income; subject to IRS contribution limits and RMDs starting at age 73. Non-qualified accounts (brokerage, IUL, annuities): funded with after-tax dollars; growth and access are taxed differently by product.
R
Rule of 72
A simple mental math formula to estimate how long it takes money to double at a given interest rate: divide 72 by the annual rate of return.
S
S&P 500 (Standard & Poor's 500)
A market index tracking the 500 largest publicly traded U.S. companies by market cap. The most widely used benchmark for U.S. stock market performance.
Segment
The 1-year period during which the index is tracked for interest crediting purposes. A new segment begins on the "sweep date".
Standard Loan
A policy loan where the collateral is transferred out of the indexed accounts into a fixed-rate loan collateral account. The loan earns a fixed (often lower) rate while the loan interest is charged. Less favorable than an index loan for accumulation purposes.
Surrender Charge
A fee charged if the policy is fully surrendered in the early years (typically years 1–10, grading to zero). Does not apply to policy loans or partial surrenders within allowed free withdrawal amounts.
Surrogate Policy
When the client cannot be insured (criminal history, insulin, etc.), a surrogate policy insures a different person (spouse, child, partner) while the original client is the OWNER and BENEFICIARY.
T
Tax-Advantaged (as it relates to IUL)
An IUL offers three layers: (1) Tax-deferred growth. (2) Tax-free distributions (policy loans). (3) Income-tax-free death benefit.
TEFRA (Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act, 1982)
One of the first major federal laws to regulate life insurance as a tax-advantaged vehicle. TEFRA established that life insurance must maintain a minimum 'corridor' — a required gap between the death benefit and the cash value.
Total Charges
The sum of all monthly deductions and premium deductions shown in the illustration. Includes COI, policy fee, per unit charge, rider charges, and asset charges.
V
Variable Universal Life (VUL)
A permanent life insurance policy where cash value is invested directly in sub-accounts (like mutual funds) — with FULL market exposure. Unlike an IUL, a VUL has NO floor. Requires a securities license to sell.
Volatile Equity Index
A market index characterized by large, unpredictable swings in value — both up and down.
Volatility (Market)
The degree to which a market index or investment fluctuates in value over time. High volatility = large, unpredictable swings up and down.
W
Withdrawal (vs. Policy Loan)
A permanent reduction of cash value and death benefit — unlike a loan, which is repaid and does not permanently reduce the policy. Withdrawals are taxed under LIFO (Last In, First Out) rules: gains come out first and are taxable as ordinary income. Early withdrawals can also jeopardize MEC status. Best practice: use policy loans, not withdrawals, for tax-free income.
